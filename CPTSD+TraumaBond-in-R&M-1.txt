One Hundred Years of Hurt: Complex PeeTee EhssDee, Trauma Bonding, and the Cage With the Open Door in Rick and Morty

A longitudinal study of Rick Sanchez and Morty Smith across nine seasons

Introduction: The Case File in the Garage

On its surface, Rick and Morty is a nihilistic science-fiction comedy: a drunk genius drags his anxious grandson through portals, the universe turns out to be indifferent, and nearly every cosmic revelation is defused with a burp and a catchphrase. But beneath the improv rhythms and the body horror, the show has been running a quieter, more disciplined experiment for nine seasons. Strip away the sci-fi furniture and what remains is a case file—a week-by-week longitudinal record of an abusive attachment, documented with a consistency that most prestige dramas never attempt. The garage at the Smith residence is both laboratory and cell. The portal gun is both a toy and a set of manacles. And the relationship at the centre of the show—the relationship the title actually names—is one of the most sustained depictions of a trauma bond ever smuggled into popular entertainment under cover of a cartoon.

This video is a study of that relationship. Its central claim is threefold.

First, that Morty Smith presents, with remarkable consistency across nine seasons, the clinical profile of Complex Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder—the condition Judith Herman described in Trauma and Recovery as the psychological consequence of prolonged, repeated trauma under conditions of captivity, and which the ICD-11 now formally recognises as distinct from ordinary Pee Tee Ess Dee. Morty is hypervigilant, dissociative, shame-saturated, and relationally damaged in ways that map, symptom by symptom, onto the presentation of a hostage—a person whose survival has depended on managing the moods of a more powerful figure he cannot escape.

Second, that the mechanism of his captivity is the trauma bond itself: a cycle of abuse, endangerment, and humiliation punctuated by unpredictable, electrifying moments of rescue, approval, and apparent sacrifice. This is the pattern psychologists call intermittent reinforcement, and it is the most powerful behavioural adhesive known to science—the same schedule that keeps a gambler at the machine and a battered partner in the house. Morty is not loyal to Rick despite the danger. He is loyal to Rick because the danger makes the rare tenderness feel like salvation.

Third, that Rick Sanchez is the show's other Complex PeeTeeEssDee case, and that the two conditions are welded together. Rick uses Morty as a cloaking device—and here the show is unusually literal, establishing in its own mythology that Ricks keep Mortys close because a Morty's brainwaves cancel out a Rick's, hiding him from his enemies. The essay will argue that this is the series' founding metaphor: Morty is emotional camouflage, a human screen behind which Rick hides from his own unprocessed grief. The captor is also a casualty. The abuse is real, and so is the wound it conceals, and the show's genius is that it never lets either fact cancel the other.

Woven through all three claims is a fourth theme - that of moral injury. The term comes from the literature on combat veterans—the work of Brett Litz and colleagues on the psychological damage done not by fear but by conscience: by perpetrating, failing to prevent, or witnessing acts that violate one's deepest moral beliefs. Morty is fourteen years old, and across nine seasons he is made to kill, to abandon, to betray, to bury his own corpse, to eat the dead, to die repeatedly for someone else's convenience—and then to sit at the breakfast table and say nothing about it. His distress mirrors the veteran's with an exactness the show eventually makes literal, when a Season 8 episode simply turns him into a traumatised ex-soldier and calls the joke by its name.

And, yes this video is applying clinical frameworks to fictional characters, but it does so as a thought experiment, not a diagnosis of real people. And the reading is not an imposition. Rick and Morty invites it openly and repeatedly: it gives its characters an actual therapist and lets her name the family sickness on camera; it builds an entire citadel on the industrialised production of Rick-and-Morty pairs; it writes episodes whose plots are, without metaphor, about memory suppression, dissociation, addiction, and the re-enactment of trauma. The show knows what it is about. What follows simply takes it at its word, season by season, scene by scene.

Working chronologically, we begin with the clinical framework, briefly and precisely. Then we'll walk the nine seasons in order, reading the key scenes—describing them, and what they mean within the framework—as the bond is installed, maintained, tested, ruptured, and re-chosen. The video closes an account of what the whole nine-season arc has been demonstrating all along, and why the show's refusal to let Morty simply leave is not a failure of nerve but the darkest and most honest thing about it.

Part One: The Clinical Framework—Naming the Wound

Complex Pee Tee Ess Dee: the signature of captivity

Ordinary PeeTeeEssDee, as classically defined, follows an event: a crash, an assault, a battle. Its signature symptoms are re-experiencing (flashbacks, nightmars), avoidance, and hyperarousal. Complex PeeTeeEssDee, as Herman formulated it in 1992, follows something else: not an event but a situation—prolonged, repeated trauma from which the victim cannot escape, inflicted within a relationship of radical power imbalance. Hostages. Prisoners. Battered partners. Abused children. The captive's problem is not a single wound, but a total environment: the need to survive, day after day, inside someone else's volatility.

The ICD-11 codifies See-PeeTeeEssDee as the PeeTeeEssDee core plus three "disturbances in self-organisation": chronic affect dysregulation (emotions that flood, or go numb, without warning); a persistently negative self-concept (toxic shame, the felt conviction of being worthless or ruined); and chronic relational disturbance (the inability to trust, to leave, or to be close without fear). Herman adds the features that matter most for our purposes: somatisation, dissociation, hypervigilance toward the captor's moods, and—crucially—identification with the aggressor, the captive's adoption of the captor's perspective as a survival strategy. If you cannot escape the dangerous person, the psyche's last resort is to become fluent in him: to anticipate him, appease him, excuse him, and finally to see the world through his eyes, because his eyes are the ones watching the door.

Hold that profile up to Morty Smith and the fit is immediate. The stammer and the sweating are the comedy surface of a boy who is never not scanning. Morty monitors Rick's intoxication level, his tone, his boredom threshold, the particular set of his mouth that means someone is about to die. He dissociates—the show gives him a literal thousand-yard stare within six episodes and then builds entire episodes (the memory archive of "Morty's Mind Blowers," the fragmented psyche of "Rick: A Mort Well Lived") out of his capacity to split off unbearable experience. His self-concept is a ruin: he apologises reflexively, assumes fault automatically, and treats his own terror as an inconvenience to others. And his relationships are organised entirely around the bond—he has no friendships that survive contact with the adventures, no ambitions Rick does not sabotage, no romantic life that Rick's errands do not destroy.

The trauma bond: intermittent reinforcement as captivity.

The term "Stockholm syndrome" entered the language in 1973, after a bank robbery in Norrmalmstorg in which hostages came to defend their captors. The clinical literature that followed—most importantly Dutton and Painter's 1981 work on battered partners—located the adhesive with precision. It is not kindness that bonds a victim to an abuser. It is intermittency: the unpredictable alternation of threat and relief, cruelty and tenderness, within a power imbalance the victim cannot correct. Lenore Walker's cycle of abuse describes the rhythm—tension builds, violence erupts, and then comes the honeymoon: the apology, the gift, the rescue, the moment of apparent love—before the tension begins again.

Behavioural science explains why this rhythm is so much stronger than consistent kindness ever could be. Ferster and Skinner demonstrated in 1957 that variable-ratio reinforcement—reward delivered on an unpredictable schedule—produces the most persistent, extinction-resistant behaviour of any conditioning pattern. It is the schedule of the slot machine. The nervous system that cannot predict when the reward is coming cannot stop pulling the lever, because the next pull might be the one. Applied to a relationship: the child who is hugged every day can leave home; the child who is hugged sometimes—after the rages, at random, never when expected—is lashed to the parent forever, because the relief of those rare moments is proportionate to the terror of the rest. The worse the lows, the more luminous the highs. Abuse does not undermine the bond; it prices the tenderness, and the price keeps rising.

Evan Stark's work on coercive control adds the final piece: modern captivity rarely requires locked doors. The cage is relational. The hostage stays because the captor has colonised the hostage's reality—his memory, his self-worth, his sense of alternatives—until the open door no longer reads as an exit. This is why the essay's title image is a cage with the door open. Morty can technically walk away from Rick at any time, and several seasons stage exactly that attempt. The bars are internal: fear, shame, erased memories, sabotaged alternatives, and a nervous system calibrated so precisely to Rick's approval that ordinary life feels colourless by comparison.

Moral injury: the veteran's wound

Moral injury, as Litz and colleagues defined it in 2009, is the damage done when a person perpetrates, fails to prevent, or witnesses acts that transgress their deeply held moral beliefs. It is distinct from Pee Tee Ess Dee: Pee Tee Ess Dee is a fear injury, moral injury is a conscience injury. Its markers are guilt, shame, demoralisation, self-sabotage, and a corroded sense of one's own goodness—and in the veteran population where it was first studied, it predicts despair more reliably than fear does. The morally injured person does not flinch at loud noises. He flinches at his own reflection.

Morty's arc is a moral-injury curriculum. He is not merely endangered; he is made complicit—made to kill, to smuggle, to lie, to abandon a world, to keep secrets from his family, to violate his own ethical instincts on a schedule, and then to absorb the consequences alone. Again and again the show constructs the same experiment: Morty's conscience fires, Morty acts on it, and the act either catastrophically backfires or is revealed as a setup, teaching him—the way a trainer teaches an animal—that conscience is a liability. The result, by the middle seasons, is a boy who can commit atrocities in a dissociated state and then sit through math class. The show will eventually stop being subtle about the veteran parallel at all.

The supporting cast of concepts

Four more terms will recur. Identification with the aggressor (Anna Freud, building on Ferenczi): the victim's adoption of the abuser's behaviours and worldview—watch for Morty beginning to sound like Rick, and eventually to do Rick to others. Betrayal trauma (Jennifer Freyd): abuse inflicted by an attachment figure is processed differently from stranger danger, because the victim's survival depends on preserving the relationship—so awareness of the betrayal is suppressed, producing "betrayal blindness." Fawning (Pete Walker's fourth trauma response, alongside fight, flight and freeze): appeasement as survival, the reflexive management of the dangerous person's emotions. And parentification: the child drafted into managing the adult's inner life, becoming the caretaker of his own caretaker. All four are load-bearing in what follows.

One last framing. None of this requires that Rick feel nothing. That is the point the discourse about abusive relationships so often misses, and the point Rick and Morty understands better than almost any text that touches the subject: trauma bonds do not form in the absence of love. They form in the presence of love that is real, intermittent, and lethal. The show's entire nine-season argument is contained in that sentence. Now to the evidence.

Part Two: Season One — The Installation of the Bond

Season One is where the machinery gets built, and it is worth pausing on how fast it happens. Within eleven episodes — roughly four hours of television — the show establishes every load-bearing element of the dynamic that the next eight seasons will simply maintain and vary: the captivity that calls itself adventure, the first moral injuries, the first burials, the first rescues, and the final revelation that the captor is screaming behind his own grin. Season One is not a warm-up. It is the induction protocol, and it runs with the efficiency of a grooming process precisely because, structurally, that is what it is.

"Pilot": the manacles that look like a toy

The first scene of the series is the thesis statement. Morty is asleep in his bed — a fourteen-year-old in his own room, in the middle of the night — when Rick bursts in, drunk, reeking, and drags him out of the house into a hovering spacecraft built out of garbage. Morty is not asked. He stammers protest the entire way, and Rick overrides every syllable of it with a torrent of manic justification: he needs Morty, it's important, there's no time. Within minutes Morty is in Dimension 35-C, being ordered to collect mega seeds while Rick monologues about their value. When Morty's legs break on a cliff fall, Rick steps through a portal and leaves him alone in an alien wilderness — and then returns with advanced medicine and heals him. Hold that sequence up to the framework from Part One and it is the entire cycle of abuse compressed into ninety seconds: endangerment, abandonment, rescue, relief. The terror of being left broken on an alien world prices the medicine Rick brings back. Morty's gratitude is manufactured by the same man who manufactured the need for it.

Then comes the smuggling. To get the seeds through interdimensional customs, Rick orders Morty to conceal them inside his own body — "way up inside your butthole, Morty" — a violation played as absurd comedy that nonetheless establishes the show's foundational transaction: Morty's body and dignity are resources Rick may spend. And at the checkpoint, when the ruse fails, Rick hands Morty a weapon and tells him the armed agents pursuing them are robots. "They're just robots, Morty! It's okay to shoot them! They're robots!" Morty fires. He kills. And then he sees the blood, hears one dying agent call out to another by name, and understands in real time that they were people — "They're not robots, Rick!" — to which Rick replies, with serene indifference, that it's a figure of speech, and that bureaucrats aren't real people anyway.

This is Morty's first moral injury, and the show timestamps it: less than one episode into the series, a fourteen-year-old has been deceived into killing, by the person he was taught to trust, in service of an errand he never chose. The Litz definition fits without alteration — Morty has perpetrated an act that transgresses his deepest moral beliefs, under the direction of an authority figure, and the guilt lands on him rather than on the man who handed him the gun. Rick experiences nothing; he is already complaining about customs. Morty stares at what he did. That asymmetry — the captor registers nothing, the captive registers everything — will never close.

And note the final beat of the episode, because it is the trapdoor the whole series is built over. The mega seeds dissolve, and for a few hours Morty is fluent, confident, brilliant at school — Rick's gift, a taste of what proximity to the great man can provide. Then it curdles: the seeds' aftereffects seize him mid-sentence and he collapses into a drooling, twitching paralysis on the bedroom floor while Rick looks down at him, unbothered, already talking about the next thing. The reward is real. The reward is also a toxin. That is intermittent reinforcement rendered as a pharmacological gag, and it is the first of a hundred variations on the theme: everything Rick gives Morty is both the prize and the poison.

"Meeseeks and Destroy": the bet, the bathroom, and the bullet

Episode five is the season's darkest chamber, and it is structured as a negotiation inside a coercive relationship — which is itself diagnostic. Morty, exhausted by the adventures, asks for a turn at the wheel: one adventure he chooses, run his way. Rick frames this as a bet, and the stakes Rick sets reveal the power geometry exactly. If Morty wins, he gets to choose every tenth adventure. If Rick wins, Morty must go on a thousand adventures "and shut up about it" — no complaints, no negotiations, total compliance. Consider what is being wagered: Morty's remaining autonomy, his right to object to his own endangerment, staked on a single outing, by a boy who has to gamble for the right to be heard. Nobody with free exit from a relationship needs to win the right to say no. The bet only makes sense inside captivity.

The adventure Morty chooses is deliberately gentle — helping a poor village, a fairy-tale errand, the kind of quest where a boy gets to be a hero — and it degrades in stages, each stage a small moral injury. Morty's good intentions get a giant killed: their ascent of the beanstalk ends with Dale the giant dead on the floor of his own home, his son wailing over the body, and Morty stammering that it was an accident while the camera makes him look at what his helpfulness cost. His conscience fires, and reality punishes it. That is the training schedule this essay identified in Part One: in Rick's world, conscience is not merely useless, it is dangerous, and Season One teaches the lesson with a dead father and a grieving child.

Then the show does something it has rarely matched since: it stops being funny for four minutes. In a toilet in the giants' world, the jolly, candy-voiced King Jellybean — a character designed, visually, to be harmless — corners Morty in a bathroom stall and attempts to rape him. The scene is not played as a gag. It is shot and paced as an assault: the door, the pleading, the struggle, the sickening specificity of Morty's begging. Morty escapes by beating his attacker away, staggers out of the bathroom shaking, voice wrecked, and tells Rick he wants to go home. He does not say why. He cannot say why. And Rick — who missed nothing, who watched the king walk into that bathroom after his grandson — simply looks at the crown Jellybean left behind and understands everything, instantly, wordlessly.

What follows is the most important three seconds of the season for our purposes, because it is the trauma bond's payoff rendered as a single image. Rick concedes the bet — a rare, unconditional gift, Morty wins — and then, as they step through the portal home, Rick pauses, aims a weapon back through the doorway, and executes King Jellybean without a word of explanation. No speech. No hug. No acknowledgment that anything happened at all. Just a bullet: I saw. I believe you. He is dead. For a boy dissolving in shame, it is the most precisely targeted act of love imaginable — protection without exposure, justice without a conversation he cannot survive having. And this is exactly the mechanism Dutton and Painter described: the low is abyssal — violation, helplessness, silence — and so the high, one bullet through a portal, lands with the force of absolution. Morty will spend the rest of the series chasing the feeling of that bullet. The show is honest enough to let the moment be genuinely moving and structurally sinister at once: the rescue is real, and it is also the hook.

"Rick Potion #9": the first burial of the self

If "Meeseeks and Destroy" injures Morty's conscience, episode six injures something more fundamental: his ontological security, his belief that the world under his feet is his. The setup is another quiet moral transgression — Morty asks Rick for a love potion to use on Jessica, which is to say Morty asks for a chemical override of another person's consent, and Rick supplies it with a shrug — and the escalation is catastrophic in the show's signature manner. The potion bonds with flu season; the infection spreads; Rick's hasty fixes compound it; within a day, every human being on Earth who is not a blood relative of Morty Smith has been mutated into a praying-mantis abomination and then, after Rick's second fix, into a shambling Cronenberg horror. The planet is gone. Civilisation, every friend, every stranger, six billion people — unmade, permanently, by a chain of events that started with Morty's wish and Rick's contempt for consequences.

And here is where the episode becomes the series' founding document on complicity. Rick does not fix it. Rick announces that he could fix it, maybe, but that fixing things is hard, and there is an easier way. The easier way is this: find a parallel dimension identical in every respect except one — a dimension where Rick and Morty solved the Cronenberg crisis and then died immediately afterwards — portal into it, and step into their vacant lives. Which means, concretely, that Morty Smith, age fourteen, must dig a grave in his own backyard and bury the corpse of Morty Smith. The scene is staged without mercy: the rain, the shovels, the two dead boys staring up out of the earth, and the live one looking down at his own face — grey, slack, dead by his grandfather's side in a world that got lucky. Rick digs with the brisk efficiency of a man who has done this before, which the later seasons will confirm he has. Morty digs like a boy performing his own funeral, because that is what he is doing.

The moral-injury structure here is layered. Morty helped cause an apocalypse — perpetration. He then abandoned that world rather than atone in it — the failure to prevent, the desertion. And then he was made to dispose of the evidence with his own hands, burying not just a body but the entire moral event: in the new dimension, none of it happened, which means none of it can ever be confessed, mourned, or resolved. There is no one he can tell. His parents remember a son who was never Cronenberged; his sister's double died on the drive home from her real family. The crime is unspeakable not because it is secret — plenty of people keep secrets — but because the world it happened in no longer exists. Morty is the sole living witness to the worst thing he ever did, and he has to sit at the breakfast table inside the alibi.

The episode's closing image is the one the whole fandom rightly canonised: Morty slumped on the sofa in the stolen living room, hollow-eyed, unblinking, while Mazzy Star's "Look on Down from the Bridge" plays — a song about a dead thing watching the living — and Rick kicks his feet up, entirely at home. Morty is dissociating on camera. That stare, the thousand-yard vacancy of a boy whose psyche has split off an experience too large to hold, is the single most clinically legible image of the season: this is what See-PeeTeeEssDee's dissociative compartment looks like when a cartoon draws it honestly. And Rick's obliviousness beside him is the other half of the portrait — the captor for whom the atrocity was a Tuesday, which is precisely what makes the atrocity untellable. How do you report a crime to your only witness when your only witness is the perpetrator?

"Rixty Minutes": the graves speech

Two episodes later, the show demonstrates what dissociation sounds like when it learns to talk. "Rixty Minutes" is mostly an improv showcase — interdimensional cable, endless sketches — but its emotional spine belongs to Summer, who has discovered that in most realities her parents' lives went better without her: Beth is a celebrated surgeon, Jerry a famous filmmaker, and Summer herself was the pregnancy that derailed both. She is packing a bag to run away when Morty intercepts her, and what he says to her is, under our framework, one of the most quietly horrifying speeches in the series — delivered, and received, as comfort.

Morty tells her the truth. He takes her into the backyard and shows her the graves. "There's my grave," he says, in the flat tone of a boy pointing out a vegetable patch. He explains that the Morty she grew up with is dead, buried twenty feet from the house, that he himself is a replacement from a ruined world, and that every morning he eats breakfast twenty feet from his own rotting corpse. And then he lands the lesson he has distilled from it: "Nobody exists on purpose. Nobody belongs anywhere. Everybody's gonna die. Come watch TV."

Summer is comforted — the speech genuinely works, she stays, the scene is warm — and that is exactly what makes it so diagnostic. Listen to who Morty sounds like. The cadence, the nihilism, the cosmic indifference worn as a shrug: it is Rick's worldview, verbatim, flowing out of his grandson's mouth. This is identification with the aggressor in its purest scripted form. The captive has survived the unsurvivable not by processing it but by adopting the perpetrator's interpretive frame — if nothing matters and everyone dies, then a boy buried in his own garden is merely a data point, and the grief never has to be felt. Anna Freud's mechanism is running in real time: Morty has made Rick's philosophy the load-bearing wall of his psyche, because the alternative — actually mourning the boy in the ground — would collapse him. And note the secondary function: Morty is using his own unprocessed trauma as a caregiving tool, managing his sister's crisis with the grave he cannot grieve. The parentified child has become the family's psychic mortar, holding everyone else together with the pieces of himself.

"Close Rick-counters of the Rick Kind": the cloaking device, named

The penultimate episode of the season is where the show stops implying its central metaphor and engraves it. Framed for the murder of a Rick from another dimension, our Rick drags Morty on the run across the multiverse, and the trail leads to the truth about why Ricks keep Mortys at all. The answer, delivered flatly as series mythology, is that a Morty's brainwaves are the inverse of a Rick's: the "genius waves" of a Rick are cancelled out by the — the show's own term — "Morty waves" of his grandson. A Morty is a cloaking device. A living, breathing, fourteen-year-old piece of counter-surveillance equipment, bred and kept to make a dangerous man invisible to the things hunting him.

The essay's third claim required no invention; the show wrote it down. Rick hides behind Morty. Not only physically — electromagnetically, canonically — but, as this study argues, emotionally: the grandson is the screen of ordinary family life behind which the most grieving mind in the universe conceals itself, the camouflage of barbecues and breakfast tables that makes a shattered man look like an eccentric grandfather. And the episode drives the metaphor to its industrial terminus. The villain's lair is a dome covered in tortured Mortys — hundreds of them, strapped to the structure, writhing in continuous agony — because a single Morty masks one Rick, but this many Mortys, kept in maximal distress, can hide something even bigger. It is the show's most literal image of its thesis: Morty-suffering as raw material, fuel, shielding. The captive's pain is the captor's cover.

Our Morty's role in the episode completes the portrait. Among the imprisoned Mortys, he is the one who organises the uprising — the Mortyest Morty, the one whose faith in Rick-ness is most developed — and the episode lets him be briefly, genuinely heroic in defence of the very system that farms him. The most bonded hostage leads the breakout and then walks back into his own cell, because his cell is the one with his Rick in it. Even the twist lands the theme: the true mastermind is Evil Morty, a Morty who seized control of his Rick from inside the bond — the captive who escaped by becoming the captor, which is less an escape than the bond's final mutation, and a shadow-path the show will hold over our Morty for the rest of its run.

"Ricksy Business": the meaning of the catchphrase

The finale supplies the season's Rosetta stone, and it gives it to a side character. The Smith house is accidentally teleported to an alien world mid-party, and amid the chaos there is a quiet exchange on the steps between Morty and Birdperson, Rick's oldest friend. Morty is fretting — fawning, in Pete Walker's precise sense; his entire role at the party has been crisis management, damage control, the anxious containment of the mess Rick made, the child caretaking the adult's consequences while the adult dances — and he tries to explain his grandfather's moods using the catchphrase the audience has been laughing at all season. Birdperson gently corrects him: on his world, "wubba lubba dub dub" is not a party noise. It means: I am in great pain. Please help me.

The season's entire comic surface inverts in one line. Every burped catchphrase, every manic cold open, every night Rick has dragged Morty out of bed trailing chaos — all of it has been a distress call, broadcast continuously, in a language no one around him bothers to translate. The captor is a casualty. The man who makes a cloaking device out of his grandson is himself hiding from grief so total it will take five more seasons to name it, and the grandson's absorption into that hiding — his recruitment as camouflage, caretaker, and interpreter of pain he is never permitted to address directly — is the full co-dependent circuit closed. Morty ends his first season as he will spend most of the next eight: frozen beside Rick, literally, in this finale's case, the two of them caught in stopped time together, cleaning up Rick's mess, the hostage and the man in pain, suspended.

That frozen tableau is the right image to carry forward. Season One installs the bond; it does not test it. Morty has been endangered, violated, made complicit in genocide, forced to bury himself, and trained to speak his captor's nihilism as mother tongue — and he has also been healed, avenged, and chosen, just often enough, on no predictable schedule, that the leash now runs on the inside. He has not yet tried to leave. The seasons ahead will stage that attempt, again and again, and each failure will teach us more about what the bond is made of. Season Two begins with Morty arguing — actually arguing, out loud, in Rick's own cadence — and the show will make clear that the boy's growing fluency in Rick is not emancipation. It is the cage redecorating itself.
